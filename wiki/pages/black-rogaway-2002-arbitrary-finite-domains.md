**Terms.** A **cipher** is a family of permutations of one set, one for each key; a **block cipher** is a cipher on the bit strings of one length `n`, and an **ideal** block cipher realizes every permutation of them with exactly one key. A **pseudorandom function (PRF)** or **permutation (PRP)** is a keyed function or permutation that an attacker asking `ν` queries cannot tell from a uniformly random one; the attacker's **advantage** is the difference between the probabilities that it answers 1 against the keyed object and against the random one.[^2]

## Summary

**The problem.** A block cipher permutes `[0, 2^n − 1]` for `n` such as 64 or 128, but many applications need a permutation of `[0, N − 1]` for some other `N`: ten-digit credit-card numbers, a field of a database whose format cannot change, or the group `Z_p^*` in the password protocol EKE.[^3] Embedding the domain in a larger one by padding is not enough. When the key space is small, an attacker who sees one ciphertext decrypts it under every candidate key and keeps the key whose result has the padding, while with a cipher on `[0, N − 1]` "Every ciphertext C, under every possible key K, would correspond to a valid message M."[^4] The security goal is that an attacker cannot tell the cipher from a uniformly random permutation of `[0, N − 1]`, even after querying almost every point.[^5]

**Method 1, the prefix cipher `Px`.** Encipher `0, 1, …, N − 1` with `E` and replace each output by its rank among the `N` outputs; the ranks are the permutation. It is uniform when `E` is ideal, and its setup takes `O(N)` time and memory and leaves a table of secret data, so it suits only small `N`, about `N < 2^30`.[^6]

**Method 2, cycle walking `Cy`.** Apply `E` to a point of the domain, and apply it again as long as the result lies outside the domain; the first point back in the domain is the ciphertext, and deciphering walks back with `E^{-1}`.[^7] Walking along the cycle of `E` through the start point always returns to the domain, so `Cy` is a permutation of `[0, N − 1]`. **Theorem 1** states that if `E` is a uniformly random permutation of a set of `N′ ≥ N` points containing the domain, then `Cy` is a uniformly random permutation of the domain. The proof adds the outside points one at a time, each inserted into one of the existing cycles or as a new fixed point, and counts the same number of permutations of the larger set over every permutation of the domain.[^8] The count is exactly `N′!/N!` (computed). Cycle walking costs an expected two calls of `E` or fewer when `N` exceeds half of `2^n`; with "extremely bad luck" one encipherment can take `N` calls.[^9] Schroeppel's Hasty Pudding cipher uses the same iteration, and Schroeppel dates the idea to rotor machines.[^10]

**Method 3, the generalized Feistel cipher `Fe[t, N_left, N_right]`.** Choose `N_left`, `N_right` with `N_left·N_right ≥ N`, and split a point `m` into `m mod N_left` and `⌊m/N_left⌋`. Each round keeps one half, adds a round function `f_j` of it to the other half modulo that half's size, and swaps the two halves, the sizes alternating between `N_left` and `N_right`; after `t` rounds the halves are recombined, and a result outside `[0, N − 1]` is walked back into the domain as in Method 2.[^11] Each round is undone by subtracting, so the cipher is a permutation for any round functions.

**Theorem 2.** With three rounds whose round functions are uniformly random maps of `[0, 2^n − 1]`, `2^n > N_left, N_right`, an attacker asking `ν` queries has advantage at most

```
((ν + N_left·N_right − N)² + ν²) / 2^{n+1} · (⌈2^n/N_left⌉ + ⌈2^n/N_right⌉)
```

against a uniformly random permutation of `[0, N − 1]`; without the `ν²` term it is the bound against a random function. The proof adapts Luby and Rackoff's analysis of three-round Feistel. The ceilings measure the bias of reducing a uniform `n`-bit number modulo `N_left` or `N_right`, and they tend to `2^n/N_left` and `2^n/N_right` as `n` grows, so the bound tends to `((ν + N_left·N_right − N)² + ν²)(1/N_left + 1/N_right)/2`.[^12] The surplus `N_left·N_right − N` enters because one encipherment may run the network on every point outside the domain before it returns.[^13] The authors advise choosing `N_left` and `N_right` close together with a small surplus, for example both near `√N`.[^14]

**What is left open.** The prefix cipher is too slow above about `2^30` points, cycle walking needs a block cipher on a domain not much larger than `N`, and Theorem 2 is weak for small domains (the paper names `N < 2^128`); the authors place the uncovered gap at about `2^30 ≤ N ≤ 2^60`, which contains the credit-card example `10^10 ≈ 2^33.2`.[^15] Section 6 also extends a cipher on `[0, N − 1]` to `[0, N]` by inserting the new point `N` into a cycle in front of a secret random point (the one-off construction), and enciphers `Z_N^*` and elliptic-curve groups by cycle walking or Method 3.[^16]

**Background.** The Feistel construction is cited to Smith's 1971 IBM report on the design of Lucifer; a balanced Feistel network turns a pseudorandom function on `n/2` bits into a block cipher on `n` bits, and Luby and Rackoff bounded its security for three and four rounds.[^17]

## The castle reading

A castle of width `w` and height `h` is a skyline `(c_1, …, c_w)` with `1 ≤ c_i ≤ h` and some `c_i = h`; there are `A(w, h) = h^w − (h − 1)^w` of them, and `V(w, h)` is the set of the even-block ones, `A^e(w, h)` of them ([[castle-notation](pages/castle-notation.md)]). The block of a block cipher is its `n`-bit input, not a block of a castle, and none of the three methods depends on a castle's block count: each enciphers any set of skylines. The numbers below take `V(w, h)`, the Project Euler set, as the domain. Read a skyline in `{1, …, h}^w` as the base-`h` number `Σ_i (c_i − 1) h^{i−1}`, column 1 the lowest digit. With `N_left = h^⌈w/2⌉` and `N_right = h^⌊w/2⌋`, the split of Method 3 is the split into the first `⌈w/2⌉` columns and the remaining `⌊w/2⌋`, so `Fe[t, h^⌈w/2⌉, h^⌊w/2⌋]` is a Feistel cipher on the left and right column halves of a skyline, each round adding a keyed function of one half to the other as base-`h` numbers with carries. Cycle walking with it as `E` enciphers any set of skylines, such as the castles of height `h` or `V(w, h)`, and the cycles of the walk are the cycles of `E` with the skylines outside the set erased.[^exec]

**The cost of the walk.** For a uniformly random permutation of `N′` points, the walk from a point of an `N`-point domain takes `(N′ + 1)/(N + 1)` calls on average (computed). From the `h^w` skylines that is `(h^w + 1)/(A + 1)` onto the castles of height `h` and `(h^w + 1)/(A^e + 1)` onto `V(w, h)`:[^exec]

| `(w, h)` | `h^w` | `A(w, h)` | `A^e(w, h)` | average calls onto the castles of height `h` | average calls onto `V(w, h)` |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| (10, 2) | 1024 | 1023 | 528 | 1.00 | 1.94 |
| (8, 3) | 6561 | 6305 | 3031 | 1.04 | 2.16 |
| (7, 4) | 16384 | 14197 | 7063 | 1.15 | 2.32 |
| (6, 5) | 15625 | 11529 | 5478 | 1.36 | 2.85 |
| (13, 10) | `10^13` | 7,458,134,171,671 | 3,729,050,610,636 | 1.34 | 2.68 |

`A^e(13, 10)` is the Project Euler value.[^pe] At `(13, 10)` the walk onto `V(13, 10)` costs `2^{1.42}` calls: 0.42 bits for the condition that some column reach `h` and one bit for the even block count, the two terms of `log₂ A^e` on [[one-bit-seminar](pages/one-bit-seminar.md)]. The bit for the block count comes from choosing the even-block domain; onto all castles of height 10 the walk costs the 0.42 bits alone, 1.34 calls.

**Theorem 2 at `(13, 10)`.** Before any query the bound is `(N_left·N_right − N)²(1/N_left + 1/N_right)/2`, so it says something only when the surplus is below `1/√(1/N_left + 1/N_right)`, about `√(N_left/2)` for halves of equal size. On all `10^13` skylines, with the halves `10^7` (columns 1 to 7) and `10^6` (columns 8 to 13) and no surplus, the bound stays at or below 1/2 up to `ν = 674 ≈ 2^9.4` queries. Taking `V(13, 10)` itself as the domain inside the `10^13` skylines, as Section 6 does for other domains, makes the surplus `6.27·10^12`, and the bound exceeds 1 before any query. Ranking `V(13, 10)` onto `[0, A^e − 1]` first ([[castle-bdd-zdd](pages/castle-bdd-zdd.md)], [[castle-samplers](pages/castle-samplers.md)] section 5) and splitting it as `1,742,569 × 2,139,973 = A^e + 1` leaves a surplus of 1 and covers `ν = 692 ≈ 2^9.4` queries, while the square split `1,931,075²` leaves a surplus of 44,989 and a bound above 1. The paper's own example, `N = 2^35` split as `185,360 × 185,368`, has surplus 74,112 and a bound of about 29,631 before any query.[^exec] [[morris-rogaway-stegers-2009-thorp-shuffle](pages/morris-rogaway-stegers-2009-thorp-shuffle.md)] covers about `2^27.7` queries at the same size.

## Key Takeaways

- **Three ciphers on `[0, N − 1]`** from a block cipher: the prefix cipher for small `N`, cycle walking when the domain fills a good part of the block cipher's domain, and a Feistel network with halves of sizes `N_left`, `N_right`.[^6][^7][^11]
- **Cycle walking loses nothing** against an ideal block cipher (Theorem 1).[^8]
- **Theorem 2** bounds three-round generalized Feistel by about `((ν + N_left·N_right − N)² + ν²)(1/N_left + 1/N_right)/2`; the surplus must be small.[^12][^13]
- **On castles,** the methods apply to any set of skylines, whatever its block counts; the halves are the column halves of a skyline, the walk at `(13, 10)` costs 1.34 calls onto the castles of height 10 and 2.68 onto the even-block ones, and the bound covers about `2^9.4` queries there.[^exec]
