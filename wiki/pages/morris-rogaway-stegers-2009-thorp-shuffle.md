**Terms.** A **cipher** is a family of permutations of `[0, N − 1]`, one for each key. In a **nonadaptive chosen-plaintext attack (nCPA)** the attacker fixes its `ν` plaintexts in advance and sees their ciphertexts; in a **chosen-ciphertext attack (CCA)** it may also ask for decryptions, each query chosen after the earlier answers. The **advantage** is how much more often the attacker answers 1 against the cipher than against a uniformly random permutation.[^2] The **total variation distance** between two distributions is the largest difference of the probabilities they give one event, and a **coupling** of two Markov chains runs them on shared randomness so that once they meet they move together; the probability that they have not met by time `t` bounds the distance at time `t`.[^3]

## Summary

**The problem.** Encipher a 9-digit number, such as a U.S. social-security number, into a 9-digit number under a shared key, built from a standard block cipher such as AES and proved as secure as it. Balanced Feistel networks and Benes networks have security that falls off at the square root of the domain size, which gives no useful guarantee at `N = 10^9`; Black and Rogaway ([[black-rogaway-2002-arbitrary-finite-domains](pages/black-rogaway-2002-arbitrary-finite-domains.md)]) found no practical provably secure method for domains of about `2^20` to `2^50` points, where an attacker can afford more than `√N` queries but an encipherment cannot afford `N` steps.[^4]

**The Thorp shuffle.** Cut a deck of `N` cards, `N` even, into two equal piles; drop the bottom card of one pile and then of the other, a fair coin deciding which pile goes first, and repeat until the piles are empty. In positions, the cards at `j` and `j + N/2`, for `j < N/2`, go to `2j` and `2j + 1` in an order set by the coin. `Th[N, t]` is `t` such rounds.[^5] Naor observed that the shuffle is oblivious: "one can trace the route of any given card in the deck without attending to the remaining cards in the deck". So the coin of the pair `(j, j + N/2)` in a round can come from a pseudorandom function of `j` and the round number, and enciphering one card costs one call per round instead of the `(N/2)·t` coins of the whole shuffle.[^6]

**The Feistel connection.** For `N = 2^n`, read a position as an `n`-bit string. A round moves the first bit to the end and flips it when the coin of the other `n − 1` bits is 1: "In this case the Thorp shuﬄe coincides with a maximally unbalanced Feistel network", one whose round function takes `n − 1` bits to one bit. `n` rounds, in which every bit is replaced once, form a **pass**.[^7]

**Theorem 1, rapid mixing.** Follow `ν` designated cards. After `r(2n − 1)` rounds of `Th[2^n, ·]`, `r ≥ 1`, the distribution of their positions is within total variation distance `(ν/(r + 1))(4nν/N)^r` of the uniform distribution on the ordered `ν`-tuples of distinct positions.[^8] The proof follows the cards one at a time (Lemma 2: the distance is at most the sum over `l < ν` of the expected distance of card `l + 1` given cards `1, …, l`). It couples the true process with one in which card `l + 1` is uniform among the free positions, both driven by the same coins. A round shifts a position one bit and writes the card's coin into the freed bit, so `n` rounds with the same coins bring the two copies of card `l + 1` to the same position, unless in either copy the card shares a pair with one of the first `l` cards (their positions differ by `N/2`) and moves by that card's coin. After the first `n − 1` rounds two cards share a pair with probability at most `2^{1−n}`, so card `l + 1` is still unmatched after `2n − 1` rounds with probability at most `2nl·2^{1−n} = 4nl/N`, and after `r` such stretches with probability at most `(4nl/N)^r`; summing over `l` gives the bound.[^9] The authors describe this as the first use of coupling to prove the security of a symmetric primitive.[^10]

**Security.** The total variation distance of Theorem 1 is the advantage of a nonadaptive chosen-plaintext attacker (Theorem 3), and the reversed shuffle obeys the same bound (Corollary 4).[^11] A theorem of Maurer, Pietrzak and Renner then gives chosen-ciphertext security at twice the rounds: `Th[2^n, r(4n − 2)]` has CCA advantage at most `(2ν/(r + 1))(4nν/N)^r` (Theorem 5).[^12] Asymptotically, `2r` passes resist `N^{1−1/r}` nonadaptive queries and `4r` passes `N^{1−1/r}` chosen-ciphertext queries, which proves a conjecture of Patarin and separates unbalanced from balanced Feistel.[^13] At `N = 2^40` with 16 passes an attacker needs at least `2^26.2` queries for CCA advantage 1/2, against `2^16.8` from Naor and Reingold's one-pass analysis of the same network and `2^18.5` from Maurer and Pietrzak's bound for balanced Feistel.[^14] Against a designated-point attack, where the attacker must say something about one point it chooses, the bound is `(4nν/N)^r` (Theorem 8), and two passes suffice asymptotically.[^15]

**Why balanced Feistel stops.** An `r`-round balanced Feistel network on `n` bits realizes at most `2^{r·(n/2)·2^{n/2}}` permutations, while a random permutation produces `N!/(N − ν)!` possible transcripts of `ν` queries. An attacker who asks `ν = 2^{θ+n/2}` queries and checks whether any round functions fit them keeps a positive advantage until the number of rounds reaches a threshold between `2^θ` and `2^{θ+1}`; at `n = 30` and `ν = 2^20`, 63 rounds leave it an advantage above `1 − 10^{−10000}`, and 64 are needed. Security proofs for ciphers built from pseudorandom functions go through information-theoretic security, so with a fixed number of rounds they cannot reach far beyond `2^{n/2}` queries.[^16] The paper also recalls that "a Feistel-determined permutation is always even [28, Th 6.1]".[^17]

**Other domain sizes and an implementation.** The theorems assume `N = 2^n`. Cycle walking ([[black-rogaway-2002-arbitrary-finite-domains](pages/black-rogaway-2002-arbitrary-finite-domains.md)]) reaches any `[0, N − 1]`, but "the cost of applying this domain transformation can be nearly as bad as an expected doubling in the encryption and decryption time", and results for every even `N` are left to the full version.[^18] Section 5 implements `Th[N, t]` for any `N` divisible by 32: one 128-bit output of the pseudorandom function supplies the coins of five consecutive rounds (the 5x trick, Theorem 10), so `t` rounds cost `t/5` calls of AES; cycle walking then extends to any `N` with at most 31 extra points. Fig. 4 tabulates the calls and the proven query counts for `N = 2^20, 2^30, 2^40`, and the construction takes a tweak and enciphers fields of different sizes under one key.[^19]

**Context.** The full Thorp shuffle mixes all `N` cards in a polylogarithmic number of rounds, `O(lg^44 N)` by Morris and later `O(lg^4 N)`; a cipher needs only the `ν` cards an attacker sees to mix.[^20] The shuffle comes from Thorp's 1973 paper on the game of Faro, and the problem is the small-domain case of format-preserving encryption.[^21]

## The castle reading

**A castle cipher with a proof.** The castles of width `w` and height `h` number `A(w, h) = h^w − (h − 1)^w`, not a power of two. Rank them onto `[0, A − 1]` by the first column of height `h` ([[song-as-castle](pages/song-as-castle.md)]), encipher with `Th[2^n, t]` for the least `2^n ≥ A`, walk back into `[0, A − 1]`, and unrank. At `(13, 10)`, `A = 7,458,134,171,671` lies between `2^42` and `2^43`, so `n = 43` and the walk takes `(2^43 + 1)/(A + 1) = 1.18` calls on average.[^exec] Sixteen passes, 688 rounds, contain `r(4n − 2) = 680` rounds for `r = 4`, and Theorem 5 keeps the CCA advantage of `Th[2^43, ·]` at or below 1/2 up to about `2^28.5` queries; the same computation at `n = 40` returns the paper's `2^26.2`.[^exec] Black and Rogaway's Theorem 2 covers about `2^9.4` queries at the same size ([[black-rogaway-2002-arbitrary-finite-domains](pages/black-rogaway-2002-arbitrary-finite-domains.md)]). [[feistel-network](pages/feistel-network.md)] sets the constructions side by side.

**Theorem 1 against the exact chain.** At `N = 64` (`n = 6`) and two designated cards, the chain on the 4032 ordered pairs of positions is small enough to run exactly. After `r(2n − 1) = 11r` rounds, the worst distance from uniform over all starting pairs is `1.2·10^{−2}`, `5.7·10^{−6}`, `3.3·10^{−9}` and `1.7·10^{−12}` for `r = 1` to 4, against bounds of 0.75, 0.375, 0.211 and 0.127.[^exec] The bound holds with a wide margin at this size.

**Shuffles and couplings on the wiki.** A Thorp round cuts the deck exactly in half and drops one card from each pile per coin; the riffle shuffle of [[bayer-diaconis-1992-dovetail-shuffle](pages/bayer-diaconis-1992-dovetail-shuffle.md)], which this paper lists among its references, cuts binomially and drops cards in proportion to the pile sizes.[^5][^22] [[castle-samplers](pages/castle-samplers.md)] uses a coupling to draw exact samples (coupling from the past); this paper uses one to bound how fast a chain mixes.

## Key Takeaways

- **The Thorp shuffle is maximally unbalanced Feistel** at `N = 2^n`, and it is oblivious, so one card is enciphered without shuffling the deck.[^6][^7]
- **Theorem 1:** after `r(2n − 1)` rounds, `ν` cards are within `(ν/(r + 1))(4nν/N)^r` of uniform, by coupling.[^8][^9]
- **Security to `N^{1−1/r}` queries** with `4r` passes against chosen-ciphertext attacks, where balanced Feistel cannot be proved beyond about `√N`.[^13][^16]
- **On castles,** rank, encipher with `Th[2^43, ·]` and unrank gives a cipher on the castles of width 13 and height 10 at 1.18 calls per encipherment, secure to about `2^28.5` queries with 16 passes.[^exec]
