# On the Group of a Sandpile (Rossin, Algorithms Seminar 2000)

**Source:** https://algo.inria.fr/seminars/sem00-01/rossin.html (cached as `raw/rossin-2000-group-of-a-sandpile.md`, a plain-text copy with the page's symbol-font characters restored from context)
**Publication:** Dominique Rossin (LIX, École polytechnique), "On the Group of a Sandpile", Algorithms Seminar (INRIA), 16 October 2000; summary by Dominique Gouyou-Beauchamps.
**Date ingested:** 2026-09-27
**Type:** seminar summary (13 references)

## Summary

The talk sets up the abelian sandpile on a connected multigraph `G` with vertices `1, …, n`, where `e_{i,j}` edges join `i` and `j`, `d_i` is the degree of `i`, and vertex `n` is the sink, which absorbs every grain that reaches it. A vertex `i < n` holding at least `d_i` grains topples: it loses `d_i` grains and each neighbor `j` gains `e_{i,j}`. Every configuration stabilizes to a unique stable configuration, and the number of topplings does not depend on their order; the summary cites this to [9], Dhar, Ruelle, Sen and Verma.[^1] A configuration is recurrent when it is stable and some nonzero addition of grains returns to it after stabilizing. With `u ⊕ v` the stabilization of `u + v`, the recurrent configurations form a finite abelian group, and its structure does not depend on which vertex is the sink. The simplest recurrent configuration is `δ`, with `d_i − 1` grains on every vertex `i < n`. With `ū = δ − u`, the identity is `δ ⊕ (δ ⊕ δ)‾`, and the inverse of a recurrent configuration `u` is `Id ⊕ (δ ⊕ u)‾`.[^2]

The summary states that `T_G(1, y)`, the Tutte polynomial of `G` at `x = 1`, is the generating function of the recurrent configurations by their number of grains.[^3] On its first example (section *Examples*), `T_G(1, y) = y² + 3y + 3`, and the seven recurrent configurations hold 3, 3, 3, 4, 4, 4 and 5 grains. Their generating function by grains is `y³·T_G(1, y)`, so there the statement holds up to the factor `y³`.[^9]

The main body is algebraic. A configuration `u` becomes the monomial `x^u = x_1^{u_1} ⋯ x_n^{u_n}`, a toppling of `i` becomes the binomial `T(x_i) = x_i^{d_i} − Π_j x_j^{e_{i,j}}`, and these binomials together with `x_n − 1` generate the toppling ideal `I_G`. The quotient of the polynomial ring by `I_G` has dimension equal to the order of the group. A toppling order is the graded reverse lexicographic order with the vertices ordered by their distance to the sink. For a toppling order, the toppling polynomials of all vertex subsets form a Gröbner basis (Theorem 1). Those of the well-connected subsets, the subsets `X` such that both `X` and its complement induce connected subgraphs, form a minimal Gröbner basis (Theorem 2). And `Φ(u) = δ − u` maps the monomials that do not reduce one-to-one onto the recurrent configurations (Theorem 3).[^4] Writing `ρ(u)` for the reduction of `u`, Proposition 1 gives the identity as `Φ(ρ(δ)) = δ − ρ(δ)`. As an algorithm: reduce `δ` by set topplings on well-connected subsets until none applies, then replace each count `n_i` by `d_i − 1 − n_i`. The summary's algorithm paragraph writes `d_i − n_i` for this last step. On the first example that gives the unstable configuration `(2, 3, 1)`, while Proposition 1 gives the identity `(1, 2, 0)`.[^4][^9] The abstract says the identity "presents fractal aspects that we are not able yet to explain".[^5]

## Examples

**A four-vertex multigraph.** Vertices 1 and 2 are joined by two edges, and 1-3, 2-4 and 3-4 by one each, with vertex 4 the sink; the degrees are 3, 3, 2, 2. The toppling polynomials are `x_1³ − x_2²x_3`, `x_2³ − x_1²x_4`, `x_3² − x_1x_4`, `x_4² − x_2x_3` and `x_4 − 1`. Besides `x_4 − 1`, the minimal Gröbner basis has six elements, one for each well-connected set `{3}, {2}, {1}, {1, 2, 3}, {1, 2}, {1, 3}`. Seven monomials do not reduce, `1, x_1, x_1², x_2, x_2², x_3, x_1x_3`, so the group has order 7. None of the seven is a recurrent configuration, but their images under `Φ` are.[^6] The images are `(2, 2, 1), (1, 2, 1), (0, 2, 1), (2, 1, 1), (2, 0, 1), (2, 2, 0), (1, 2, 0)` on vertices 1, 2, 3, which are exactly the recurrent configurations, and the reduced Laplacian has determinant 7.[^9]

**The 2 × 2 grid.** Four cells in a `2 × 2` square, each joined to the sink by two edges. The summary quotes the group from [9], Dhar, Ruelle, Sen and Verma: `Z/24 × Z/8`. From the Gröbner basis, `x_4` has order 24 and every element is `x_3^i x_4^j` with `0 ≤ i ≤ 7` and `0 ≤ j ≤ 23`, so the group has order 192 and two generators.[^7]

## Key Takeaways

- **The sink does not matter for the group.** The group structure is independent of the sink vertex; the wiki's sink model fixes the bottom-left cell as the sink ([[sandpile-group](pages/sandpile-group.md)]). The identity element itself does depend on the sink ([[sandpile-identity](pages/sandpile-identity.md)]).[^2]
- **The identity recipe.** `Id = δ ⊕ (δ ⊕ δ)‾` is the recipe of [[sandpile-identity](pages/sandpile-identity.md)], written there `stab(2m − stab(2m))` with `m = δ` the fullest stable configuration.[^2]
- **The 2 × 2 grid is the dual of the castle `(3, 3, 3)` (the wiki's reading).** The castle graph of the `3 × 3` square has four bounded faces, one inside each of its four `2 × 2` blocks of cells. Its planar dual has one vertex per block, joined where two blocks share an edge of the castle graph, and one vertex for the outer face, which each corner block meets along two edges. So the dual is the talk's `2 × 2` grid with every cell joined twice to the sink. Its group, `Z/24 × Z/8`, equals the sink group of `(3, 3, 3)` computed from the cells on [[sandpile-group](pages/sandpile-group.md)] (`Z/8 × Z/24`, 192 recurrent configurations).[^7][^9] The summary lists Cori and Rossin's paper "On the sandpile group of dual graphs" in its references by title only.[^8]
- **Tutte and Gröbner.** `T_G(1, y)` and the grain counts of the recurrent configurations agree up to a factor on the first example, and the reduced monomials with `Φ` give a second algorithm for the identity.[^3][^4][^9]
