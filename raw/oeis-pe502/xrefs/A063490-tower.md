## Identity and proof

Offset 1 matches the castle count with no shift: a(n) counts castles of width 4 with n blocks
(the bottom row is one of them) and 5-cores with n - 1 corners.

The proof is the A160747 one (`A160747-tower.md`, "Identity and proof") with t = 5. A castle
of width 4 with column heights c(1), ..., c(4) >= 1 has c(1) + Sum_{i=2..4} max(0, c(i) - c(i-1))
blocks. Cho et al., Theorem 3.3, send a 5-core with m corners to its bead counts
(0, n_1, ..., n_4), with Sum_{i=1..5} |n_i - n_{i-1}| = 2m and n_5 := 0, that is,
n_1 + Sum_{i=2..4} max(0, n_i - n_{i-1}) = m. So c(i) = n_i + 1 is a castle with m + 1 blocks.
Proposition 3.4 with t = 5 and m = n - 1 is

    cc(5, n-1) = Sum_{i=1..min(n-1,2)} N(n-1,i) * binomial(2n+3-2i, 2n-2),        n >= 2,

which equals (2n-1)(7n^2-7n+6)/6, the entry's name, as polynomials (checked symbolically).
a(1) = 1 is the castle of height 1, the bottom row alone (the empty partition). On p. 14 the
paper itself points to A063490 for t = 5.

The first formula is the g.f.: x(1+x)(1+5x+x^2) = x(1 + 6x + 6x^2 + x^3), the coefficients
being (N(4,1), ..., N(4,4)) = (1, 6, 6, 1), and the coefficient of x^n in x^k/(1-x)^4 is
binomial(n+3-k, 3). A castle of width 4 is a bargraph whose semiperimeter is its width plus
its number of blocks, 4 + n, hence a(n) = A271942(n+4, 4).
